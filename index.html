SECTION 1 — Introduction & Electric Cells

The study of electric charge falls into two categories: static electricity (charges at rest) and current electricity (charges in motion). Electric current is a flow of electric charge through a conductive medium — typically moving electrons in a wire, or ions in an electrolyte.

The Simple Electric Cell

An electric cell transforms chemical energy into electrical energy. The simplest cell uses two different metals (electrodes) dipped in an electrolyte solution.

Fig. — Simple electric cell: Zinc and Carbon electrodes in dilute H₂SO₄

The chemical reaction: Zn + H₂SO₄ + H₂O → ZnSO₄ + H₂O + H₂

A battery is a combination of several cells. The emf E of a cell is the total energy per coulomb it delivers to the external circuit (unit: volts, V).

SECTION 2 — Electric Current & Current Density

I = ΔQ / Δt    [Unit: ampere, A = C/s]
I = ΔQ/Δt = qnAvd
J = I / A = qnvd    [Unit: A/m²]
Direction: Current density J is in the direction of positive charge motion. For electrons, J is opposite to electron drift direction.

Worked Example — Drift Velocity: Copper wire (2mm×2mm, 4m long) carries 10A. n=8×10²⁸ m⁻³. A=4×10⁻⁶ m², J=2.5×10⁶ A/m², vd=J/(nq)=1.953×10⁻⁴ m/s

SECTION 3 — Ohm's Law, Conductance & Resistivity

I ∝ V → I = V/R or V = IR
G = 1/R = I/V   [Siemens, S]
R = ρL / A   [ρ in Ω·m]
σ = 1/ρ   (Ω·m)⁻¹   J = σE

Temperature Dependence

R = R₀ [1 + α(T − T₀)]

For metals: resistance increases with T (positive α). For semiconductors: resistance decreases with T (negative α).

SECTION 4 — Ohmic vs Non-Ohmic Conductors

Ohmic: Obey Ohm's Law, I–V graph is straight line through origin (copper, tungsten, CuSO₄).

Non-Ohmic: Do NOT obey Ohm's Law, I–V graph is a curve or doesn't pass through origin (diode, battery, neon gas).

SECTION 6 — Internal Resistance & Terminal Voltage

V = E − Ir  (discharging)
V = E + Ir  (charging)
I = E / (R + r)

Worked Example: Battery emf=4V, r=2Ω, R=8Ω → I=4/(8+2)=0.4A, Terminal p.d.=0.4×8=3.2V

SECTION 7 — Electrical Power & Efficiency

P = VI = I²R = V²/R
η = V/E = R/(R+r)
P_out = E²R/(R+r)²
Maximum Power Transfer: Maximum power when R=r. Efficiency at that point = 50%. For higher efficiency use R≫r.

SECTION 9 — Kirchhoff's Rules

KCL (Junction Rule): ΣI = 0 at any junction. Based on conservation of charge.

KVL (Loop Rule): Σ(E+IR) = 0 around any closed loop. Based on conservation of energy.

Sign Conventions: Resistor traversed with current → −IR (drop); opposite → +IR (rise). Cell from − to + → +E; + to − → −E.

Worked Example: 10V and 5V batteries, 2Ω and 3Ω resistors in single loop: −2I+10−5−3I=0 → 5I=5 → I=1A

§ 14.1–14.3 — Magnetic Field & Magnetic Lines of Force

A magnetic field is a region of space around a magnet, moving charge, or current-carrying conductor where magnetic force can be detected. Symbol: B⃗. SI unit: Tesla (T) = N/(A·m) = Wb/m².

Formal Definition: B⃗ at a point is defined by the force on a positive test charge q moving with velocity v⃗. Fmax=qvB when velocity ⊥ field.

Properties of Magnetic Field Lines

  1. Emerge from North, enter South outside the magnet; South to North inside — closed loops.
  2. Never intersect (unique direction at each point).
  3. Tangent = field direction; density indicates strength.
  4. Perpendicular to conductor surface at that surface.
  5. Exist in 3D; tend to shorten; repel laterally.

§ 14.4 — Magnetic Force on a Current-Carrying Wire

F = BIl sin θ

Maximum force when θ=90° (Fmax=BIl). Zero when parallel. Direction by right-hand rule.

Right-Hand Grip Rule: Thumb = conventional current direction; curled fingers = magnetic field direction encircling the wire.

§ 14.5 — Magnetic Force on a Moving Charge

F⃗ = qv⃗ × B⃗
F = qvB sin θ

Force is ⊥ to both v⃗ and B⃗. For ⊥ entry: circular motion.

r = mv/(qB)
ω = qB/m  |  T = 2πm/(qB)  |  f = qB/(2πm)
Key Insight: Period T and angular speed ω are independent of particle's speed and orbital radius — they depend only on q/m and B.

§ 14.6 — Cyclotrons

K.E = ½mv² = (q²B²/2m)r²

Cyclotron frequency f=qB/(2πm) is constant. K.E ∝ q², B², and 1/m.

§ 14.7 — The Hall Effect

VH = vBd
VH = IBd/(nqA)

Hall coefficient = 1/(nq). Used to determine carrier type and density in semiconductors.

§ 14.8 — Torque on a Current Loop

τ = BIA  (single loop)
τ = BIAN  (N-turn coil)

Principle behind electric motors, analog voltmeters, and ammeters.

§ 14.11 — The Biot-Savart Law

dB⃗ = (μ₀/4π)·(Idl⃗ × r̂)/r²

μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A (permeability of free space).

Key results:

Long wire: B = μ₀I/(2πa)
Solenoid: B = μ₀nI = μ₀NI/l
Circular loop centre: B = μ₀I/(2r)
Loop axial: B = μ₀IR²/[2(x²+R²)^(3/2)]

§ 14.12 — Forces Between Parallel Conductors

F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd)

Same direction → attract. Opposite → repel.

Definition of the Ampere: Current in each of two infinitely long parallel wires 1m apart producing 2×10⁻⁷ N/m force on each wire.
